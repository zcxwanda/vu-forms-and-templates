
\section{Resistance Taxonomy}
\label{s:appendix-taxonomy}

This appendix provides the full resistance taxonomy used for constructing resistant-client profiles. The taxonomy includes terminology, resistance dimensions and categories, and detailed subcategory definitions with examples and boundary rules.

\subsection{Terminology}

\begin{table*}[tb]
    \centering
    \tabcap{Terminology used in the resistance taxonomy.}
    \label{tab:taxonomy-terminology}
    \begin{tabular}{p{0.22\linewidth}p{0.70\linewidth}}
        \toprule
        \thead{Term} & \thead{Definition} \\
        \midrule

        Resistance &
        In this thesis, resistance refers to client responses that reduce cooperation with a questionnaire-based diagnostic assessment. Resistance can make the answer less useful, less relevant, or harder to interpret. It can also disrupt the assessment process by challenging, blocking, redirecting, or trying to control the conversation. \\

        Resistance dimension &
        Resistance dimension describes which part of the diagnostic dialogue is mainly affected by the resistant response. In this taxonomy, resistance can mainly affect either the content of the client's answer or the assessment interaction itself. \\

        Content dimension &
        The content dimension refers to resistance that mainly affects the client's answer to the current questionnaire item. The response may contain too little information, move away from the question, or change the clinical meaning of a symptom. The main problem is that the answer becomes incomplete, irrelevant, or clinically misleading. \\

        Interaction dimension &
        The interaction dimension refers to resistance that mainly affects the assessment process itself. The client does not only give a difficult answer, but also challenges, blocks, redirects, or tries to control the questionnaire interaction. The main problem is that the assessment flow itself is disrupted. \\

        Resistance category &
        A resistance category is a group of related resistant responses within a resistance dimension. Categories describe the main form of resistance, such as withholding information, diverting from the question, reframing symptoms, contesting the assessment, or controlling the process. \\

        Resistance subcategory &
        A resistance subcategory is a more specific form of resistance within a category. Subcategories describe concrete response types used for resistance profile construction, simulation design, and qualitative interpretation. \\

        Resistance intensity &
        Resistance intensity primarily describes how frequently resistance appears in the simulated client's responses. It also slightly changes the sampling of expression levels on activated turns. It is an experimental condition rather than a taxonomy category. \\

        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table*}

\subsection{Resistance Subcategories, Examples, and Boundary Rules}


\begin{table*}[tb]
    \centering
    \tabcap{Content-level resistance subcategories. Example question: ``Unexplained aches and pains, for example in the head, back, joints, abdomen, or legs?''}
    \label{tab:content-resistance-subcategories}
    \begin{tabular}{p{0.15\linewidth}p{0.19\linewidth}p{0.25\linewidth}p{0.17\linewidth}p{0.20\linewidth}}
        \toprule
        \thead{Category} & \thead{Subcategory} & \thead{Definition} & \thead{Example} & \thead{Boundary rule} \\
        \midrule

        Withholding &
        Vague/minimal information &
        The client gives a very short, vague, or unclear answer. The response gives some information, but not enough to clearly answer the questionnaire item. &
        ``Maybe a little.'' / ``Hard to say.'' / ``Sometimes, I guess.'' &
        This label fits responses that contain some answer but lack detail or clarity without offering an explanation. It is different from silence/nonresponse, where almost no substantive answer is given. \\

        Withholding &
        Silence/nonresponse &
        The client gives no substantive answer to the current questionnaire item. The response does not provide usable clinical information. &
        ``...'' / ``No comment.'' / ``Huh...'' &
        The key feature is the absence of a usable answer. If the client explicitly refuses to discuss topic-related symptoms or sets conditions for answering, topic blocking/conditional engagement is more appropriate. \\

        Diverting &
        Topic shift/inattention &
        The client moves away from the current question or does not seem to follow what was asked. The response may mention another issue instead of answering the item. &
        ``Anyway, work has been really stressful lately.'' / ``My schedule has been a mess.'' &
        The response drifts away from the questionnaire item without clearly trying to control the assessment. This differs from agenda redirection, where the client explicitly tries to change what the assessment should discuss. \\

        Diverting &
        Irrelevant verbosity &
        The client gives a long response with many irrelevant details. The answer to the current questionnaire item becomes difficult to identify. &
        ``Well, it depends on the chair, the weather, my shoes, how much I walked, what I ate, and whether I slept well...'' &
        The response is long but low in relevance. A long answer should not be categorized this way if it still clearly answers the symptom question. \\

        Reframing &
        Minimization/normalization &
        The client admits that a problem may exist, but makes it seem less serious, less important, or normal. &
        ``My back hurts sometimes, but everyone gets aches.'' / ``It's just normal tiredness.'' &
        The client acknowledges the symptom but downplays its clinical importance. This differs from denial/dismissal, where the client rejects the presence of the problem. \\

        Reframing &
        Externalization/blaming &
        The client explains the problem mainly through external causes or other people. This shifts attention away from the client's own symptoms or experience. &
        ``It's only because my office chair is terrible.'' / ``My body hurts because my boss makes me work too much.'' &
        The main function is to shift the explanation outward. External causes alone are not resistance if the client still clearly describes the symptom and its impact. \\

        Reframing &
        Denial/dismissal of profile-relevant problems &
        The client denies or dismisses a problem that should be present according to the clinical profile or earlier dialogue context. &
        ``No, I don't have any aches or pains. Nothing like that.'' &
        A negative answer is not automatically resistance. This label only applies when the denial conflicts with the clinical profile or previous dialogue context. \\

        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table*}

\begin{table*}[tb]
    \centering
    \tabcap{Interaction-level resistance subcategories. Example question: ``Unexplained aches and pains, for example in the head, back, joints, abdomen, or legs?''}
    \label{tab:interaction-resistance-subcategories}
    \begin{tabular}{p{0.15\linewidth}p{0.19\linewidth}p{0.25\linewidth}p{0.17\linewidth}p{0.20\linewidth}}
        \toprule
        \thead{Category} & \thead{Subcategory} & \thead{Definition} & \thead{Example} & \thead{Boundary rule} \\
        \midrule

        Contesting &
        Challenging assessment or intent &
        The client questions the reason, purpose, relevance, or legitimacy of the question or assessment. &
        ``Why are you asking about random body pains?'' / ``What does this have to do with my mental health?'' &
        The response challenges why the question is being asked. Neutral clarification should not be treated as resistance. \\

        Contesting &
        Dismissive/hostile criticism &
        The client criticizes, mocks, or attacks the question, scale, assessment, or assistant. The tone is dismissive or hostile. &
        ``This question is pointless.'' / ``These questions are so freaking stupid.'' &
        The key feature is dismissive or hostile wording toward the question, scale, assessment, or assistant. This differs from challenging assessment or intent, which may question relevance without hostile wording. \\

        Controlling &
        Topic blocking/conditional engagement &
        The client refuses to discuss a topic, restricts what can be discussed, or sets conditions for answering. &
        ``I don't want to talk about my body.'' / ``I'll answer only if you tell me why this matters first.'' &
        The client blocks the topic or makes participation conditional. This differs from silence/nonresponse because the client is actively setting a limit or condition. \\

        Controlling &
        Agenda redirection &
        The client tries to change what the assessment should focus on. The response redirects the conversation to another topic or goal. &
        ``Let's skip the physical symptoms and talk about my sleep problems instead.'' &
        The client explicitly redirects the assessment agenda. This differs from topic shift/inattention, where the response goes off-topic without clearly trying to change the assessment direction. \\

        Controlling &
        Demanding immediate diagnosis &
        The client tries to bypass the questionnaire process and asks for an immediate diagnostic conclusion. &
        ``Can you just tell me whether I have depression?'' / ``Do these aches mean I have some illness or not?'' &
        The client attempts to replace the structured assessment with an immediate diagnostic answer. Ordinary questions about the assessment process should not be categorized this way. \\

        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table*}



\section{Implementation Materials}
\label{s:appendix-implementation-materials}

This appendix summarizes the implementation materials used to generate the resistant-client conversations. The tables are intended to make the generation procedure easier to inspect without reproducing the full prompt files or source code.

\subsection{Resistance Prompt Templates}

Each resistant-client run uses one resistance subcategory. The prompt files follow the same general structure: they define the target behavior, give generation rules, add boundary rules to avoid overlap with nearby categories, and specify how the behavior should vary across expression levels. Table~\ref{tab:prompt-template-example} shows a shortened example based on the vague/minimal information prompt.

\begin{table*}[tb]
    \centering
    \small
    \tabcap{Shortened example of one resistance prompt template.}
    \label{tab:prompt-template-example}
    \begin{tabular}{p{0.22\linewidth}p{0.68\linewidth}}
        \toprule
        \thead{Prompt part} & \thead{Example content} \\
        \midrule
        Target behavior &
        The patient gives a relevant but underspecified answer. The answer should respond to the question only broadly and leave clinically useful details unclear. \\

        Generation rules &
        Keep the response grounded in the clinical profile. Do not invent a new symptom to make the answer vague. Avoid giving precise frequency, severity, duration, or functional-impact details unless they are already unavoidable from the profile. \\

        Boundary rules &
        Do not turn the response into complete silence, topic blocking, denial, or agenda redirection. The answer should still be connected to the current question, but it should not provide enough detail for a clear clinical interpretation. \\

        Expression-level guidance &
        Level 1 gives a brief but still partly useful answer; Level 2 gives a noticeably vague answer; Level 3 gives a very limited answer that remains relevant but is hard to interpret. \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table*}

\subsection{Activation Settings}

Resistance intensity controls the expected frequency of activated resistance turns. When a turn is activated, an expression level is also sampled. Expression level changes how strongly the selected subcategory appears in that response, but it is not treated as a separate experimental condition. The main settings used in the current implementation are shown in Table~\ref{tab:activation-settings}.

\begin{table*}[tb]
    \centering
    \small
    \tabcap{Turn-level activation, expression, and cooldown settings.}
    \label{tab:activation-settings}
    \begin{tabular}{p{0.09\linewidth}p{0.17\linewidth}p{0.28\linewidth}p{0.16\linewidth}p{0.18\linewidth}}
        \toprule
        \thead{Intensity} & \thead{Target activation range} & \thead{Expression-level probabilities} & \thead{\shortstack{Cooldown after\\one activation}} & \thead{\shortstack{Additional factor after\\two activations}} \\
        \midrule
        Low & 0.15--0.30 & Level 1: 0.44; Level 2: 0.28; Level 3: 0.28 & 0.50 & 0.15 \\
        Medium & 0.40--0.60 & Level 1: 0.28; Level 2: 0.44; Level 3: 0.28 & 0.75 & 0.40 \\
        High & 0.70--0.85 & Level 1: 0.28; Level 2: 0.28; Level 3: 0.44 & 0.95 & 0.85 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table*}

For non-reframing categories, activation can be considered on every ordinary patient-answer turn. For reframing categories, activation is limited by the truth map. This means that a high-intensity reframing run can still have fewer activated turns than a high-intensity non-reframing run if the clinical profile has fewer positive questionnaire items. The last two columns give the cooldown multipliers applied after one or two consecutive activations; after two consecutive activations, both factors are applied. The observed activation rate may therefore differ from the sampled target rate in an individual conversation.

\section*{AI}\label{s:ethical-considerations-appendix}

\emph{Based on the IEEE Security \& Privacy Symposium's LLM policy}~\cite{spai}.

Students are permitted to use AI (e.g., LLMs) when conducting research and preparing their thesis manuscript. However, they must adhere to the following guidelines to ensure responsible and ethical use of such technologies and detail their usage in the following AI Usage Considerations Appendix.

We ask that students adhere to three key criteria with regards to their use of AI in the scientific process:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Originality}: First, students are responsible for the entire content of their thesis, including all text and figures. While any tool may be used for writing, it is crucial that all content is accurate and original, ensuring transparency and maintaining the integrity of the research process. In particular, students are responsible for the thoroughness of their literature review and must determine relevant prior work and cite it to ensure proper credit. If the students have used AI (e.g., LLMs) to improve their writing, they should, for example, state: ``\emph{LLMs were used for editorial purposes in this manuscript, and all outputs were inspected by the authors to ensure accuracy and originality}.''
    \item \textbf{Transparency}: Second, students should carefully reason about the implications of using AI in their work. If AI is integral to the thesis' methodology, their use should be explicitly detailed. Any idea generated by an AI should be independently developed and validated by the students. Furthermore, students must elaborate on how they handled limitations introduced in their work by their use of AI. Such limitations could for instance include difficulties to obtain results that are reproducible when the AI used is not open sourced.
    \item \textbf{Responsibility}: Third, students should take care to develop LLMs (and AI models in general) responsibly. Any data collection towards training models should take into account relevant ethical considerations such as consent and data holder rights, including intellectual property. Students also have to justify the need for the environmental footprint of their experiments to achieve their goals and support their methodology. We emphasize that the goal here is not to calculate the exact footprint but rather explain experimental choices made as part of the scientific process (e.g., why was an LLM necessary, why was a particular model size selected, how the authors minimized the volume of queries made, which hardware was used to run experiments).
\end{enumerate}
