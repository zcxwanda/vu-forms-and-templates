\section{Experimental Setup}\label{s:experimental-setup}
The experiment is organized around four research questions.

\parab{RQ1: Conversation quality.}
How does the presence of profile-based resistant clients influence overall conversation quality? Additionally, how does performance vary across different resistance categories?

\parab{RQ2: Diagnostic quality.}
How does the presence of profile-based resistant clients influence overall diagnostic quality? Additionally, how does performance vary across different resistance categories?

\parab{RQ3: Relationship between conversation and diagnosis.}
What is the association between conversation quality and diagnostic quality?

\parab{RQ4: Conversation characteristics.}
How does the presence of profile-based resistant clients influence conversation characteristics? Additionally, how do conversation characteristics vary across different resistance categories?

RQ1 and RQ2 use the existing DSM5AgentFlow evaluation outputs, and RQ3 links the results of the former two questions. RQ4 adds client-side and descriptive analysis because the original evaluator does not directly measure whether the simulated patient actually produced the intended resistant behavior.


\subsection{Dataset and Materials}

\paragraph{Grounding Materials.}
All conversations in this study are based on the DSM-5 Level 1 Cross-Cutting Symptom Measure, a 23-item screening questionnaire that covers broad domains such as mood, anxiety, sleep, cognition, psychosis-related experiences, and substance use. The same questionnaire is used in both cooperative and resistant-client conditions, so the assessment content stays constant while the client behavior changes.

The patient agent answers questions according to the clinical profile files included in the DSM5AgentFlow codebase. I use five profiles in the main experiment: depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder, bipolar disorder, and schizophrenia. Each profile specifies the symptoms and background information that should be treated as true for that simulated patient. When the diagnostic agent writes the final report, it can retrieve information from the local DSM-5 document collection included in DSM5AgentFlow, such as APA disorder fact sheets.

The resistance materials consist of the taxonomy described in Appendix~\ref{s:appendix-taxonomy}. The taxonomy contains 12 resistance subcategories grouped into broader categories and dimensions. In resistant-client runs, a clinical profile is combined with one resistance subcategory and one intensity setting. For reframing-related subcategories, I also use profile-specific truth maps. These truth maps mark which questionnaire items are supported by the clinical profile, so that denial, minimization, and externalization are applied only to symptoms that are actually present in the simulated case.

\paragraph{Main Simulated Conversation Dataset.}
The main dataset uses a controlled factorial design. For each of the five clinical profiles, I generate one cooperative baseline condition and resistant-client conditions across 12 resistance subcategories and three intensity settings: low, medium, and high. Each condition is repeated five times. This gives a planned dataset size of $5 \times 5 \times (12 \times 3 + 1) = 925$ conversation logs.

Each log is one simulated DSM-5 assessment conversation. It contains the assistant--patient dialogue, the clinical observation, the final diagnostic output, and metadata describing the experimental condition. For resistant-client conversations, the metadata records the resistance dimension, category, subcategory, intensity, activation information, and turn-level generation information. Cooperative baseline logs use the same clinical profiles and questionnaire, but do not include a resistance profile.

\paragraph{Exploratory Stress-Test Dataset.}
In addition to the main dataset, I use a smaller stress-test dataset to examine an extreme version of the same resistant-client behaviors. In this setting, resistance is activated on every questionnaire turn, and each activated turn uses high intensity with expression level 3. This condition is not treated as another ordinary intensity level in the main factorial design. Instead, it is used as a robustness check to inspect whether the diagnostic pipeline behaves differently when resistant behavior is frequent and strongly expressed across the interview. For reframing-related resistance, the main stress-test version keeps the truth-map constraint so that reframing is still tied to profile-supported symptoms.

\subsection{Resistance Activation and Truth Maps}

Resistant runs use a turn-level activation mechanism. Before each patient response is generated, the system decides whether the selected resistance profile should be added to the patient prompt for that turn. If resistance is
not activated, the patient receives only the ordinary clinical-profile instruction. Because the resistance instruction is added only on activated turns, the resistance condition does not permanently replace the clinical patient profile. Non-activated turns are generated from the clinical profile alone, which helps preserve profile-grounded clinical information throughout the conversation.

The assigned intensity is implemented through both programmatic controls and prompt-level instructions. It affects the target activation range, the distribution from which expression levels are sampled, and an additional intensity-specific prompt appended to activated turns. Low, medium, and high intensity therefore control the expected frequency and strength of resistant behavior across a conversation, rather than forcing every answer to be resistant. When a turn is activated, an expression level is sampled to vary how strongly the selected resistance style is expressed in that response. The expression level is used as a generation device only; the main experimental condition remains the assigned resistance intensity. A cooldown mechanism is used to reduce unnatural repetition by making it less likely that the same resistance instruction is applied too densely in adjacent turns.

Some resistance types require additional profile grounding. Reframing behaviors, including minimization, externalization, and denial, are only meaningful when the current questionnaire item refers to a symptom that should
be present in the assigned clinical profile. For these sub-categories, a profile-specific truth map is used to identify profile-positive questionnaire items. The truth map prevents the patient from denying, minimizing, or
explaining away symptoms that are not part of the case, which would create profile-ungrounded clinical content. As a result, reframing conditions can have a lower observed activation rate than other resistance categories: resistance is only applied on turns where the questionnaire item is eligible according to the truth map. Additional implementation materials, including resistance prompt-template summaries, truth-map examples, activation settings, and dialogue excerpts, are provided in Appendix~\ref{s:appendix-implementation-materials}.

\subsection{Conversation Logs and Metadata}

Each generated conversation is saved as a chat log. The log contains the assistant and patient turns, the clinical observation, the final diagnosis, and metadata describing the experimental condition. For resistant conversations, the log records the resistance dimension, category, sub-category, and intensity.

The log also stores turn-level generation metadata for patient responses, including whether resistance was activated and which expression level was used. These labels are stored for analysis only. They are not shown to the assistant agent during the conversation and are not used by the diagnostic agent when it generates the final diagnosis. This keeps the diagnostic task realistic: the
diagnostic agent must diagnose from the dialogue content rather than from hidden experimental labels.

\subsubsection{Original DSM5AgentFlow Metrics}

The original evaluator is kept because it provides continuity with the upstream DSM5AgentFlow system. For conversation quality, the evaluator scores DSM-5 coverage, clinical relevance, logical consistency, diagnostic
justification, empathy/professionalism, and an average rubric score. These metrics mainly measure whether the conversation remains clinically coherent, professionally conducted, and useful for assessment.

For diagnostic quality, the evaluator reports the predicted diagnosis, diagnosis match, diagnosis classification, diagnostic justification, and
classification confidence. These metrics measure whether the final diagnostic output matches the expected clinical profile and whether the diagnostic explanation is plausible given the conversation. 

\subsubsection{Client-Side Post-Hoc Metrics}

The original evaluator mainly evaluates the assistant side and the final diagnosis. However, resistant-client simulation also needs to check whether the client behavior itself was successfully generated. For this reason, this thesis adds four client-side post-hoc metrics.

Resistance fidelity measures whether activated resistant turns match the target resistance sub-category. This metric is computed only for turns where resistance was activated. Question responsiveness measures how directly the patient answer responds to the assistant's current question. Information yield measures how much usable clinical information the patient answer provides,
including whether the answer contains enough relevant information for the assessment rather than only words or peripheral detail. Diagnostic certainty measures how confident or tentative the final diagnostic text appears.

Question responsiveness and information yield are computed both over all patient turns and over activated resistant turns. The activated-turn version shows the local effect of resistance, while the all-turn version shows the overall effect on the full conversation. This distinction is useful because a conversation may contain several resistant turns while still providing enough clinical information elsewhere for the diagnostic agent to make a correct diagnosis.

\subsubsection{Deterministic Conversation Characteristics}

In addition to rubric-based and LLM-judged metrics, the analysis includes deterministic text features derived directly from the chat logs. These features include the mean length of patient answers, the mean length of activated resistant answers, the rate of very short answers, and the rate of nonresponses. These features are not written back into the original chat logs because they are post-hoc descriptive statistics rather than part of the generation process.

These descriptive features are mainly used for RQ4. They help identify surface differences between resistance categories, such as silence/nonresponse producing short or empty answers and irrelevant verbosity producing unusually
long answers. They are interpreted together with information yield because answer length alone does not indicate usefulness: a long answer may still be clinically unhelpful if it is off-topic or filled with irrelevant details.


\subsection{Implementation Details}
The experiments are implemented by modifying the DSM5AgentFlow codebase. These modifications allow the patient agent to use resistance profiles, apply resistance through turn-level activation, save resistance metadata in the chat logs, and support post-hoc evaluation of client-side behavior. The main dialogue flow, questionnaire structure, and diagnostic pipeline are otherwise kept close to the original system so that cooperative and resistant-client conditions remain comparable.

The final experiments use Gemini 2.5 Flash as the LLM backend for the assistant agent, the patient agent, and the diagnostic agent during dialogue generation. The original DSM5AgentFlow evaluator and the post-hoc client-side evaluator are also run with Gemini 2.5 Flash. Keeping the model fixed across cooperative and resistant conditions reduces the chance that observed differences are caused by different model backends rather than by the resistance condition. The exploratory stress-test conversations are generated with a separate script that forces activation and expression level settings without changing the main experimental generation procedure.
