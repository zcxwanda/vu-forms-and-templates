\section{Results}\label{s:results}

This section reports the results of the resistant-client simulation experiment.
The results are organized around the research questions introduced in
Section~\ref{s:experimental-setup}. I first describe the evaluated dialogue set
and check whether the resistance manipulation worked as intended. I then report
conversation-quality results, diagnostic-quality results, the relation between
conversation and diagnostic quality, and descriptive conversation
characteristics.

\subsection{Evaluated Dialogue Set}

The current analysis uses 925 generated conversations. This includes 25
cooperative baseline conversations and 900 resistant-client conversations. The
resistant-client conversations are balanced across the three resistance
intensity settings: 300 low-intensity conversations, 300 medium-intensity
conversations, and 300 high-intensity conversations. Each resistant intensity
condition covers the same five clinical profiles and the same twelve resistance
subcategories. This structure allows the analysis to compare the cooperative
baseline with resistant-client conditions while also comparing different
resistance intensities and resistance categories.

\subsection{Resistance Manipulation Check}

Before interpreting the downstream evaluation scores, I first checked whether
the generated patient behaviour reflected the intended resistance condition.
This check is important because the experiment depends on the patient agent
actually producing resistance when a resistant profile is activated.

The post-hoc resistance-fidelity scores were high across all three intensity
conditions. On a five-point scale, the mean fidelity score was 4.90 for low
intensity, 4.89 for medium intensity, and 4.91 for high intensity. This suggests
that the generated resistant turns usually matched the intended resistance
subcategory rather than drifting into unrelated behaviour.

The activation rate also increased with the intended intensity. Low-intensity
conversations had resistance in 17.0\% of patient turns, medium-intensity
conversations had resistance in 36.9\% of patient turns, and high-intensity
conversations had resistance in 59.4\% of patient turns. This shows that the
intensity mechanism successfully changed how often resistance appeared in the
conversation.

The lower activation rate for reframing categories is expected. These categories
use the truth map and are only activated when the questionnaire item asks about a
symptom that is present in the clinical profile. In contrast, withholding,
diverting, controlling, and contesting resistance can be expressed on a wider
range of questionnaire items, so their activation rates are closer to the target
range for each intensity condition.

The manipulation check also shows that increasing resistance intensity reduced
the amount of usable information available in the dialogue. The overall
information-yield score decreased from 4.43 in the low-intensity condition to
3.80 in the medium-intensity condition and 3.06 in the high-intensity condition.
This pattern supports the basic assumption of the experiment: stronger
resistance does not only change the surface style of patient responses, but also
changes how much clinically useful information the conversation provides.
