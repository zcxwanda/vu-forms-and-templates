\section{Results}\label{s:results}

This section first checks whether the intended resistance was generated and then answers the four research questions. In the tables, accuracy and activation rates are proportions from 0 to 1. The remaining evaluation measures use five-point scales, where a higher value indicates more of the property named by the measure. For the statistical tests, $p<.05$ indicates evidence of a difference, while effect sizes closer to zero indicate a weaker difference. All 1,225 conversations have complete conversation-quality, diagnostic-quality, responsiveness, and information-yield scores. Resistance fidelity is undefined for the 25 baseline conversations and for five low-intensity reframing conversations with no activated turns. Fidelity analyses therefore use the remaining 1,195 resistant conversations.

\subsection{Resistance Manipulation Check}

The observed activation rate increased as intended, from 0.170 at low intensity to 0.370 at medium, 0.594 at high, and 0.820 at extreme. Under extreme intensity, non-reframing activation reached 1.0, while reframing activation remained at 0.278 because it was limited to truth-map-positive items.

Resistance fidelity remained stable across conditions, with mean scores between 4.89 and 4.94 (Table~\ref{tab:manipulation-check}). The manipulation therefore changed how often resistance appeared while preserving a close match between generated responses and the assigned subcategory.

\subsection{Conversation Quality (RQ1)}

Resistance mainly affected how directly patients answered questions and how much useful information they provided. Responsiveness decreased from 4.98 in the baseline condition to 3.21 at high intensity and 2.45 at extreme intensity. Information yield followed a similar pattern, decreasing from 4.97 to 3.06 and then to 2.33. The overall conversation-rubric score changed less, from 2.78 at baseline to 2.32 at high intensity and 2.23 at extreme intensity (Figure~\ref{fig:rq1-intensity}).

The overall tests confirmed differences in all three measures (Table~\ref{tab:rq12-intensity-tests}). Pairwise tests then showed that responsiveness and information yield differed at every adjacent step from baseline to extreme intensity (all Holm-adjusted $p<.001$). The effect was weakest for the conversation rubric and strongest for information yield. Thus, resistance had a clearer effect on information gathering than on the broader conversation-quality score.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/results/conversation_quality_by_intensity.pdf}
\caption{Core conversation-quality measures by resistance intensity. Error bars show bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals.}
\label{fig:rq1-intensity}
\end{figure}

The categories also produced different results (Table~\ref{tab:rq1-category}). Reframing had the highest conversation-rubric mean and information yield, but its mean activation rate was only 0.168, compared with approximately 0.59 for the other categories. Its stronger scores may therefore result partly from having more non-resistant turns, rather than from reframing itself being less disruptive.

Because reframing was activated much less often, it was excluded from a second category comparison. The remaining four categories had similar activation rates, but differed in conversation-rubric scores, responsiveness, and information yield.\footnote{Category tests excluding reframing: activation, $H(3)=0.16$, $p=.984$, $\epsilon^2=.000$; conversation rubric, $H(3)=75.97$, $p<.001$, $\epsilon^2=.081$; responsiveness, $H(3)=79.40$, $p<.001$, $\epsilon^2=.085$; information yield, $H(3)=38.43$, $p<.001$, $\epsilon^2=.040$.} Pairwise tests showed the specific pattern. Withholding and diverting scored higher than contesting and controlling on the conversation rubric and information yield. Withholding and diverting did not differ from each other on these measures, nor did contesting and controlling. For responsiveness, withholding scored highest, followed by diverting; contesting and controlling received similarly low scores. These results show that categories affected conversations in different ways even when resistance appeared equally often.

\subsection{Diagnostic Quality (RQ2)}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.68\textwidth]{figs/results/diagnostic_quality_by_intensity.pdf}
\caption{Classification and text-match accuracy, diagnostic justification, and diagnostic certainty by intensity. Error bars show bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals.}
\label{fig:rq2-intensity}
\end{figure*}

Diagnostic accuracy and diagnostic certainty followed different patterns (Figure~\ref{fig:rq2-intensity}). Accuracy was robust from baseline through high intensity, even though certainty declined. The overall classification test detected a small difference across these conditions ($p=.039$, $V=.095$), but none of the pairwise differences remained significant after Holm correction. Text-match accuracy also did not differ reliably across these conditions (Table~\ref{tab:rq12-intensity-tests}).

In contrast, diagnostic certainty declined from 4.32 in the baseline to 3.72 at high intensity. The baseline--high pairwise difference remained significant after correction ($p=.014$), with a rank-biserial effect size of .312. Diagnostic justification also decreased, from 4.84 in the baseline to 4.41 at high intensity (Table~\ref{tab:rq12-intensity-tests}).

Extreme resistance marked a clear break from the low-to-high pattern. Classification accuracy fell from 0.963 at high to 0.583 at extreme. The accuracy difference was 0.380 (95\% CI [0.319, 0.439], Holm-adjusted $p<.001$). Text-match accuracy showed a similar drop from 0.970 to 0.637. Diagnostic certainty decreased further to 3.30. Thus, accuracy remained robust through high intensity while the diagnostic language became less certain; under extreme resistance, both accuracy and certainty declined further.

Across all resistant conditions, classification accuracy ranged from 0.830 for contesting to 0.987 for reframing (Table~\ref{tab:rq2-category}). However, this comparison includes reframing's much lower activation rate. After reframing was excluded, primary classification accuracy did not differ across the other four categories. Text-match accuracy, diagnostic justification, and diagnostic certainty did differ, although their effect sizes were small.\footnote{Category tests excluding reframing: classification accuracy, $\chi^2(3)=0.72$, $p=.869$, $V=.028$; text-match accuracy, $\chi^2(3)=11.35$, $p=.010$, $V=.112$; diagnostic justification, $H(3)=42.06$, $p<.001$, $\epsilon^2=.044$; diagnostic certainty, $H(3)=52.82$, $p<.001$, $\epsilon^2=.056$.} Pairwise comparisons showed that withholding received lower justification scores than each of the other non-reframing categories. Diverting received higher certainty scores than withholding, contesting, and controlling. Category therefore had a clearer effect on how the diagnostic conclusion was supported and expressed than on whether the primary classification was correct.

Diagnostic accuracy also differed by category under extreme intensity ($\chi^2(4)=63.89$, $p<.001$, $V=.462$). Reframing retained classification accuracy of 0.973, while the other category means ranged from 0.400 to 0.480. This contrast should not be interpreted as a pure category effect: extreme reframing was still restricted to truth-map-positive turns, whereas the other categories were activated on every questionnaire turn.

Several misclassified conversations from the extreme condition were reviewed as examples of diagnostic failure. In one PTSD conversation in the silence condition, the patient did not endorse the symptom, but the report treated the questionnaire item as evidence.

The same report inferred several other symptoms from similarly uninformative answers and assigned schizoaffective disorder, bipolar type. Other inspected conversations showed a related problem in which the resistance itself was interpreted diagnostically. In a depression conversation with topic-shifting responses, tangential and concrete answers were used to support a provisional autism-spectrum diagnosis. In an anxiety conversation with hostile criticism, irritability, agitation, and argumentative language were treated as mixed features supporting a provisional bipolar-I diagnosis. Together, these examples show that the diagnostic report may mistake either the questionnaire wording or the intended resistance for evidence about the patient's disorder. These examples do not establish a general pattern; identifying recurring error categories would require a separate systematic analysis of misclassified reports.

\subsection{Association Between Conversation and Diagnostic Quality (RQ3)}

Patient-side conversation quality was positively associated with both the overall conversation rubric and diagnostic certainty. Across all conditions, information yield correlated with the conversation-rubric mean at $\rho=.50$, $p<.001$, and with diagnostic certainty at $\rho=.39$, $p<.001$. The associations remained significant when extreme was excluded ($\rho=.44$ and $\rho=.26$, respectively; both $p<.001$).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/results/diagnostic_outcomes_by_information_yield_quartile.pdf}
\caption{Descriptive diagnostic outcomes across information-yield quartiles.}
\label{fig:rq3-quartiles}
\end{figure}

Classification accuracy was analyzed separately because each diagnosis was coded as either correct or incorrect. The regression included resistance condition and clinical profile as control variables. In this adjusted model, conversations with higher information yield were more likely to be classified correctly (OR=3.64, 95\% CI [2.65, 5.01], $p<.001$). This relationship was not statistically significant when the extreme condition was removed (OR=1.74, 95\% CI [0.89, 3.38], $p=.103$). However, only 17 of the 925 baseline-to-high conversations were misclassified. This strong ceiling effect left too little variation to estimate the relationship reliably.

Table~\ref{tab:rq3-associations} summarizes these associations across all conditions and in the baseline-to-high subset.

Figure~\ref{fig:rq3-quartiles} shows the same pattern visually by grouping conversations into four information-yield quartiles. The lowest quartile had classification accuracy of 0.631, text-match accuracy of 0.666, and diagnostic certainty of 3.14. In the second through fourth quartiles, classification accuracy ranged from 0.940 to 0.990. The quartile comparison does not separate the association with information yield from differences in resistance condition or clinical profile. It is therefore used to visualize the trend, while the adjusted regression above provides the statistical test.

\subsection{Conversation Characteristics (RQ4)}

Table~\ref{tab:rq4-category} shows different response characteristics across the resistance categories. Withholding had the shortest answers and was the only category with substantial short-answer and nonresponse rates. Diverting had the longest answers, driven by irrelevant verbosity. Contesting and controlling remained close to the baseline answer length but had the lowest responsiveness and information yield. Reframing remained close to the baseline on most measures, consistent with its lower truth-map-limited activation rate. The category values are averaged across the four resistance intensities.

These category differences remained when reframing was excluded from the comparison. Answer length, short-answer rate, and nonresponse rate all differed across the other four categories.\footnote{Category tests excluding reframing: answer length, $H(3)=503.39$, $p<.001$, $\epsilon^2=.558$; short-answer rate, $H(3)=666.83$, $p<.001$, $\epsilon^2=.741$; nonresponse rate, $H(3)=391.51$, $p<.001$, $\epsilon^2=.434$.} Pairwise comparisons showed that withholding produced shorter answers and more short or absent responses than each of the other categories. Diverting produced longer answers than each of them (all Holm-adjusted $p<.001$).

Intensity produced different changes within the individual subcategories (Table~\ref{tab:rq4-targeted-intensity}). From low to extreme intensity, vague or minimal responses decreased from 25.69 to 6.00 words, while the nonresponse rate for silence increased from 10.6\% to 96.2\%. Irrelevant verbosity increased from 48.45 to 249.07 words. Responsiveness for topic shift, both contesting subcategories, and all three controlling subcategories fell from above 4 to approximately 1. All nine comparisons remained significant after Holm correction (all $p<.001$), with effect sizes from $\epsilon^2=.869$ to $.951$. Reframing was excluded because its truth map restricts activation to profile-positive questionnaire items.
