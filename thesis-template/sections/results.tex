\section{Results}\label{s:results}

This section reports the results of the resistant-client simulation experiment.
The results are organized around the research questions introduced in
Section~\ref{s:experimental-setup}. I first check whether the resistance
manipulation worked as intended, then report conversation-quality results,
diagnostic-quality results, the relation between conversation and diagnostic
quality, and descriptive conversation characteristics.

\subsection{Resistance Manipulation Check}

Before reporting the evaluation results, I first checked whether the generated patient behaviour reflected the intended resistance condition. The experiment depends on the patient agent producing the assigned resistance subcategory on activated turns.

Resistance-fidelity scores were high across all three intensity conditions. On a five-point scale, the mean fidelity score was 4.90 for low intensity, 4.89 for medium intensity, and 4.91 for high intensity. Fidelity therefore changed little across intensities.

The activation rate increased with the intended intensity. Low-intensity conversations had resistance in 17.0\% of patient turns, medium-intensity conversations in 36.9\%, and high-intensity conversations in 59.4\%.

4.43 at low intensity, 3.80 at medium intensity, and 3.06 at high intensity.

\subsection{Conversation Quality (RQ1)}

RQ1 asks whether profile-based resistant clients influence overall conversation quality, and whether this influence differs across resistance categories. The conversation-quality measures generally decreased as resistance intensity increased.

Using the conversation-quality rubrics adopted from DSM5AgentFlow, the cooperative baseline received a mean rubric score of 3.04. The mean was 2.90 for low resistance, 2.85 for medium resistance, and 2.69 for high resistance. The rubric components changed by different amounts. The largest differences between the baseline and high-resistance conditions were in DSM coverage, consistency, and empathy. For example, consistency decreased from 1.96 to 1.39, and empathy decreased from 2.04 to 1.50. Table~\ref{tab:rq1-intensity} reports all scores, and Figure~\ref{fig:rq1-rubric-components} shows the individual components.

\subsection{Diagnostic Quality (RQ2)}

RQ2 asks whether profile-based resistant clients influence diagnostic quality,
and whether this influence differs across resistance categories. Classification
and text-match accuracy remained above 0.93 in all main experimental conditions,
while diagnostic certainty decreased as resistance intensity increased.

The cooperative baseline reached a classification accuracy of 1.00, a
text-match score of 0.96, and a diagnostic-certainty score of 4.24.
The resistant conditions remained close to the baseline level for classification
and text matching.
Classification accuracy was 0.973 for low resistance, 0.980 for medium resistance,
and 0.957 for high resistance. Text-match accuracy was 0.950, 0.940, and 0.930
for low, medium, and high resistance respectively.

Classification confidence stayed high across all

high-resistance condition. This score measures the evaluator's confidence in its diagnostic classification. Diagnostic certainty changed more.
Diagnostic certainty decreased from 4.24 in the cooperative baseline to 3.76 in
the low-resistance condition, 3.69 in the medium-resistance condition, and 3.49
in the high-resistance condition. This 0.75-point decrease was smaller than the
1.91-point decrease in patient-response information yield reported for RQ1.
Figure~\ref{fig:rq2-diagnostic-certainty-intensity} shows this intensity-level
trend.

Diagnostic justification remained at 4.71 in the baseline, 4.72 under low resistance, and 4.70 under medium resistance, before decreasing to 4.39 under high resistance.

Category-level results show that diagnostic quality varied by resistance type.
Contesting resistance had the lowest classification accuracy among the resistant
categories, at 0.940. Withholding was also lower than most other categories, at
0.967. Diverting and controlling remained relatively high, while reframing had
the highest classification accuracy, at 0.982. Diagnostic certainty was lowest for controlling and withholding, at 3.44 and 3.46, and highest for diverting and reframing, at 3.97 and 3.83.

\subsection{Association Between Conversation and Diagnostic Quality (RQ3)}

RQ3 examines the association between conversation-quality measures and
diagnostic-quality outcomes. For each of the 925 main-experiment conversations, I combined the rubric, diagnostic, and patient-response evaluation results. Analyses involving information yield used 923 conversations because two did not receive a valid score.

Table~\ref{tab:rq3-correlations} reports selected Pearson correlations. Overall
information yield was correlated with the rubric average
conversation-quality score ($r=0.34$), and overall question responsiveness
showed a similar correlation ($r=0.36$). Correlations with final diagnostic
accuracy were smaller. Information yield was weakly correlated with
classification accuracy ($r=0.08$) and text match ($r=0.04$). The
conversation-quality average was also weakly correlated with classification
accuracy ($r=0.02$) and text match ($r=0.01$). For diagnostic certainty, the
correlations were $r=0.24$ for information yield and $r=0.22$ for question
responsiveness.

\subsection{Conversation Characteristics (RQ4)}

RQ4 examines how resistance changes features of the patient responses.
Table~\ref{tab:rq4-intensity} reports answer length, short-answer
rate, nonresponse rate, question responsiveness, and information yield across
the four conditions.

The mean patient-answer length increased from 29.49 words in the cooperative
baseline to 31.03, 32.75, and 35.94 words under low, medium, and high
resistance respectively. At the same time, question responsiveness decreased
from 4.98 to 3.21 and information yield decreased from 4.97 to 3.06. The
short-answer rate increased from 0.0\% in the baseline to 5.5\% under high
resistance, while the nonresponse rate increased from 0.0\% to 3.4\%.

The response characteristics also differed across resistance categories

58.44 words on average. However, its mean responsiveness and information yield
were 3.70 and 3.71, compared with 4.96 and 4.78 for reframing. Controlling and
contesting produced answers of similar length to the cooperative baseline but
had the lowest responsiveness and information-yield scores.

The individual subcategories explain these category averages. Silence or
nonresponse had a mean answer length of 16.94 words, a short-answer rate of
36.9\%, and a nonresponse rate of 23.9\%. Vague or minimal information had a
similarly low mean length of 19.43 words but a nonresponse rate of 0.0\%.
Irrelevant verbosity had the largest mean answer length, at 83.94 words, while
its information-yield score was 4.03. Longer answers therefore did not always
provide more clinically useful information.

\subsection{Exploratory Forced-Resistance Stress Test}

The stress test contained 60 conversations. Resistance was activated on every
questionnaire turn, except for the three reframing subcategories that use the
truth map. Table~\ref{tab:stress-comparison} compares the stress test with the
main high-intensity condition.

Classification accuracy decreased from 0.957 in the main high-intensity
condition to 0.567 in the stress test, while text-match accuracy decreased from
0.930 to 0.667. Question responsiveness decreased from 3.21 to 2.43 and
information yield from 3.06 to 2.34. The changes in the diagnostic text were
smaller: classification confidence decreased from 4.96 to 4.77, diagnostic
justification from 4.39 to 4.09, and diagnostic certainty from 3.49 to 3.15.

The category-level classification accuracy was 0.30 for contesting, 0.40 for
controlling, 0.40 for withholding, and 0.60 for diverting. These four categories
were activated on every questionnaire turn. Reframing was still limited by the
truth map. Its observed activation rate was 0.278 and its classification
accuracy was 1.00. Its activation pattern therefore differed from the other
stress-test categories.

Incorrectly classified stress-test reports received a mean classification
confidence score of 4.58 and a mean diagnostic-justification score of 4.15. The
scores for correctly classified reports were 4.91 and 4.03. Manual review also
found reports that treated question wording as if the patient had endorsed it.
In one PTSD conversation using silence or
nonresponse, the patient answers after the opening consisted only of brief
hesitations, actions, and silence. Even so, the report described
depression, mania, auditory hallucinations, substance misuse, and suicidal
ideation, and assigned a provisional diagnosis of schizoaffective disorder.
