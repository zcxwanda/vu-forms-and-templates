\section{Results}\label{s:results}

This section reports the results of the resistant-client simulation experiment.
The results are organized around the research questions introduced in
Section~\ref{s:experimental-setup}. I first check whether the resistance
manipulation worked as intended, then report conversation-quality results,
diagnostic-quality results, the relation between conversation and diagnostic
quality, and descriptive conversation characteristics.

\subsection{Resistance Manipulation Check}

Before interpreting the downstream evaluation scores, I first checked whether the generated patient behaviour reflected the intended resistance condition. This check is important because the experiment depends on the patient agent actually producing resistance when a resistant profile is activated.

The post-hoc resistance-fidelity scores were high across all three intensity conditions. On a five-point scale, the mean fidelity score was 4.90 for low intensity, 4.89 for medium intensity, and 4.91 for high intensity. This suggests that the generated resistant turns usually matched the intended resistance subcategory rather than drifting into an unrelated subcategory.

The activation rate also increased with the intended intensity. Low-intensity conversations had resistance in 17.0\% of patient turns, medium-intensity conversations had resistance in 36.9\% of patient turns, and high-intensity conversations had resistance in 59.4\% of patient turns. This shows that the intensity mechanism successfully changed how often resistance appeared in the
conversation.

The lower activation rate for reframing categories is expected. These categories use the truth map and are only activated when the questionnaire item asks about a
symptom that is present in the clinical profile. In contrast, withholding, diverting, controlling, and contesting resistance can be expressed on a larger number of questionnaire items, so their activation rates are closer to the target range for each intensity condition.

The manipulation check also shows that increasing resistance intensity reduced the amount of usable information available in the dialogue. The overall
information-yield score decreased from 4.43 in the low-intensity condition to 3.80 in the medium-intensity condition and 3.06 in the high-intensity condition.
This pattern supports the basic assumption of the experiment: stronger resistance does not only change the surface style of patient responses, but also changes how much clinically useful information the conversation provides.

\subsection{Conversation Quality (RQ1)}

RQ1 asks whether profile-based resistant clients influence overall conversation quality, and whether this influence differs across resistance categories. The results show a clear decrease in conversation quality as resistance becomes more frequent and more difficult to manage.

Using the original DSM5AgentFlow conversation-quality rubrics, the cooperative baseline received the highest overall rubric score, with a mean score of 3.04. The low-resistance condition was slightly lower at 2.90, the medium-resistance condition was 2.85, and the high-resistance condition was 2.69. The decrease is not large on every original rubric, but it is consistent with the expected direction. The largest changes are visible in DSM coverage, consistency, and empathy. For example, consistency decreased from 1.96 in the baseline to 1.39 in
the high-resistance condition, and empathy decreased from 2.04 to 1.50. Table~\ref{tab:rq1-intensity} gives the corresponding values, and Figure~\ref{fig:rq1-rubric-components} visualizes the same pattern across the individual rubric components.

The client-side post-hoc metrics show the effect more directly. The cooperative
baseline was close to the ceiling, with a mean question-responsiveness score of
4.98 and a mean information-yield score of 4.97. These scores decreased as
resistance intensity increased: question responsiveness decreased to 4.53, 3.92,
and 3.21 for the low-, medium-, and high-resistance conditions respectively.
Overall information yield showed the same pattern, decreasing to 4.43, 3.80,
and 3.06. This means that stronger resistance did not only change the tone of
the conversation; it also made patient answers less directly responsive and less
clinically useful. Figure~\ref{fig:rq1-client-usability} shows this monotonic
decrease in the client-side metrics.

The category-level results show that different forms of resistance affected conversation quality in different ways. Contesting and controlling resistance had the lowest overall rubric scores, with mean scores of 2.60 and 2.72. These categories also had low post-hoc responsiveness and information-yield scores, which suggests that argumentative or agenda-controlling clients make the assessment interaction less productive. Diverting and withholding resistance showed intermediate effects. Reframing had the highest overall rubric score among the resistant categories, at 3.03, and also had the highest overall information yield. This is partly because reframing was activated less often due to the truth-map eligibility rule, and partly because reframing responses often still contain some clinically relevant information even when the client minimizes, denies, or externalizes symptoms.

Overall, RQ1 is supported: resistant-client simulation reduced conversation quality, especially when resistance was more frequent and when the resistance category directly interrupted the assessment structure. However, the effect was stronger in the client-side metrics than in the original DSM5AgentFlow rubrics. This suggests that the original evaluator captures some degradation in the conversation, but it is not fully sensitive to client-side resistance behaviours such as withholding information, redirecting the conversation, or challenging the assessment.

\subsection{Diagnostic Quality (RQ2)}

RQ2 asks whether profile-based resistant clients influence diagnostic quality,
and whether this influence differs across resistance categories. Compared with
conversation quality, the diagnostic results were more stable. Resistant-client
simulation did not cause a large collapse in diagnostic performance, although
some decline is visible under high-intensity resistance and in specific
resistance categories.

The cooperative baseline reached a classification match score of 1.00, a
text-match score of 0.96, and a post-hoc diagnostic-certainty score of 4.24.
The resistant conditions remained close to the baseline level for classification
and text matching.
Classification match was 0.973 for low resistance, 0.980 for medium resistance,
and 0.957 for high resistance. Text match showed a similar but slightly noisier
pattern, with scores of 0.950, 0.940, and 0.930 for low, medium, and high
resistance respectively. These results suggest that the diagnostic agent was
often still able to recover the correct broad diagnosis even when the
conversation became less cooperative.

The original evaluator's confidence score stayed very high across all
conditions, decreasing only from 5.00 in the baseline to 4.96 in the
high-resistance condition. This means that the evaluator usually classified the
diagnosis as correct with high confidence. However, the post-hoc diagnostic-certainty metric shows a different pattern.
Diagnostic certainty decreased from 4.24 in the cooperative baseline to 3.76 in
the low-resistance condition, 3.69 in the medium-resistance condition, and 3.49
in the high-resistance condition. In other words, the final diagnostic text
became somewhat less certain as resistance increased, but this decrease was
modest compared with the stronger decline in client-side information yield
reported for RQ1. Figure~\ref{fig:rq2-diagnostic-certainty-intensity} shows
this intensity-level trend.

Category-level results show that diagnostic quality varied by resistance type.
Contesting resistance had the lowest classification match among the resistant
categories, at 0.940. Withholding was also lower than most other categories, at
0.967. Diverting and controlling remained relatively high, while reframing had
the highest classification match, at 0.982. This is consistent with the RQ1
results: categories that disrupt the structure of the assessment or challenge
the interaction appear more harmful to diagnosis than reframing, which often
still leaves some usable clinical information in the dialogue.

Overall, RQ2 is only partially supported. Resistant clients did not strongly
reduce the system's primary diagnostic accuracy, especially when enough core
symptom information remained available. However, high-intensity resistance and
contesting-style resistance showed the clearest diagnostic degradation. The
combination of high diagnostic accuracy and high evaluator confidence also
suggests that the diagnostic pipeline is robust in this setting, but may not be
very sensitive to the uncertainty introduced by resistant or incomplete patient
answers.


\subsection{Association Between Conversation and Diagnostic Quality (RQ3)}

RQ3 examines the association between conversation-quality measures and
diagnostic-quality outcomes. The paired analysis matched the original evaluator outputs with the post-hoc evaluator outputs for all 925 main-experiment conversations. Analyses involving information yield used 923 conversations, because two conversations did not receive a valid information-yield score.

Table~\ref{tab:rq3-correlations} reports selected Pearson correlations. Overall
information yield was correlated with the original evaluator's average
conversation-quality score ($r=0.34$), and overall question responsiveness
showed a similar correlation ($r=0.36$). Correlations with final diagnostic
accuracy were smaller. Information yield was weakly correlated with
classification match ($r=0.08$) and text match ($r=0.04$). The original
conversation-quality average was also weakly correlated with classification
match ($r=0.02$) and text match ($r=0.01$). For diagnostic certainty, the
correlations were $r=0.24$ for information yield and $r=0.22$ for question
responsiveness.

3.88, while classification match remained high in all quartiles.

Overall, RQ3 shows that lower conversation quality was only weakly associated with final diagnostic correctness, but was more clearly associated with diagnostic certainty. The diagnostic agent often recovered the correct broad diagnosis even from lower-quality resistant conversations, while the certainty of the final diagnostic text was more sensitive to reduced information yield.
