Large language model (LLM) agents can generate controlled mental health assessment dialogues from predefined clinical profiles, but these simulations commonly assume that the client answers are cooperative. This thesis studies how profile-based client resistance affects a questionnaire-driven multi-agent diagnostic workflow. The study builds on the DSM5AgentFlow assessment workflow and introduces 12 resistant-client subcategories covering withholding, diverting, reframing, contesting, and controlling responses. Resistance is controlled at the turn level and evaluated under low, medium, and high intensity, together with an extreme stress test, while the clinical profiles and assessment questionnaire remain fixed.

In this study, we used 1,225 simulated conversations across five clinical profiles. We evaluated conversation quality, diagnostic quality, resistance fidelity, and patient-response characteristics using rubric scores and directly computed measures. Across all intensity conditions, conversations in which resistance was activated at least once achieved a mean resistance-fidelity score of 4.89 or higher out of 5. Inferential analyses show that resistance has larger effects on question responsiveness and information yield than on the broader conversation rubric. Classification accuracy remains above 0.96 from low through high intensity, while diagnostic certainty declines. In the extreme stress test, classification accuracy falls from 0.963 to 0.583 and diagnostic certainty decreases to 3.30. These results show that resistance can reduce the information collected even when the final classification remains correct. When resistant responses dominate the assessment, both information collection and classification performance decline. Illustrative inspection also shows that a diagnostic report may infer symptoms from responses that provide little usable evidence.
