\section{Background}\label{s:background}

DSM5AgentFlow provides the base workflow for this study. This section describes its agents, the clinical profiles used to simulate patients, and the resistance taxonomy used in the experiments.

\subsection{DSM5AgentFlow and Agents}

DSM5AgentFlow is a multi-agent LLM workflow for questionnaire-based mental health assessment and explainable mental disorder diagnosis~\cite{ozgun2025trustworthyaipsychotherapymultiagent}. In this system, a patient agent generates responses from a clinical profile, an assistant agent conducts the assessment, and a diagnostic agent produces a final diagnostic report from the completed dialogue.

For this thesis, the workflow provides a controlled setting in which the questionnaire and clinical profile remain unchanged while the patient agent's response behavior is varied. Cooperative and resistant conditions can therefore be compared under the same assessment structure.

The original setting assumes a cooperative simulated patient. The patient agent answers the assistant's questions and provides information according to the assigned clinical profile. This produces useful baseline dialogues but does not show how the system behaves when the client is vague, avoidant, challenging, or otherwise difficult to assess.


\subsection{Clinical Profiles and Profile Grounding}

In DSM5AgentFlow, simulated patients are generated from clinical profiles. A clinical profile specifies the target disorder, relevant symptoms, and sometimes the response style of the simulated client. In this thesis, the clinical profile serves as the grounding source for patient generation: clinically meaningful answers should remain consistent with the assigned profile.

Because the diagnostic agent uses the generated dialogue as evidence, symptoms that are not supported by the clinical profile may change the resulting diagnosis. Such additions would also make it difficult to determine whether an observed effect was caused by resistance or by changes to the clinical case. This issue is particularly relevant to resistance that denies or reframes symptoms. Section~\ref{s:methodology} describes the grounding controls used for these subcategories.


\subsection{Client Resistance}

In this thesis, resistance is defined through patterns in the patient's answers. The taxonomy describes what the patient does during the conversation, such as withholding information or challenging the assessment. It does not attempt to explain why a real client might show the same behavior.

\subsection{Resistance Dimensions and Categories}

The resistance taxonomy used in this thesis distinguishes between two broad dimensions: content-dimension resistance and interaction-dimension resistance. Content-dimension resistance affects the information provided in response to the current questionnaire item. Examples include vague or minimal answers, silence, topic shifting, irrelevant verbosity, minimization, externalization, and denial. These behaviors mainly influence how much usable symptom information is available to the assistant and diagnostic agent.

Interaction-dimension resistance affects the assessment process itself. Examples include challenging the assessment, hostile criticism, topic blocking, agenda redirection, and demanding an immediate diagnosis. These behaviors may not only reduce the information available in a particular answer, but also interfere with the questionnaire-driven structure of the dialogue.

Content-dimension resistance changes the completeness and interpretability of client-provided information. Interaction-dimension resistance can additionally disrupt the flow and control of the assessment process.
